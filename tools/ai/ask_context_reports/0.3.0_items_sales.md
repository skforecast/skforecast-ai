### [grounded] For hourly demand with strong daily and weekly seasonality, when should I prefer a direct forecasting strategy over a recursive one?

- Skills: choosing-a-forecaster; 9.0s

For hourly demand, you should prefer a direct forecasting strategy when the predictive relationships change significantly across the forecast horizon, such as needing different models for predicting 1 hour ahead versus 24 hours ahead. It is also preferred for long-horizon forecasts where you want to completely avoid the propagation of errors, since recursive models can accumulate errors as their own predictions feed back into subsequent steps. However, this choice requires you to specify a fixed forecast horizon during model initialization.

## Key Reasons to Choose a Direct Strategy

*   **Horizon-Dependent Patterns:** For hourly demand, the relationship between past lags and future demand often changes depending on how far ahead you are predicting. A direct strategy trains an independent model for each specific step (such as step 1, step 24, or step 168), allowing each model to specialize in the unique patterns of that specific hour.
*   **No Error Propagation:** Recursive forecasting uses its own intermediate predictions as inputs for subsequent steps, which can lead to compounding errors over long horizons like daily (24 hours) or weekly (168 hours) spans. A direct forecaster uses only observed historical data to make its predictions for each step, eliminating this error accumulation.

## Trade-offs of the Direct Strategy

*   **Fixed Forecast Horizon:** Unlike recursive forecasters which offer flexible horizons at prediction time, a direct forecaster requires you to define the exact number of steps during initialization.
*   **Computation and Memory Cost:** Training a direct forecaster is slower and requires more memory. While a recursive forecaster trains only one model, a direct forecaster trains N independent models (where N is your forecast horizon, meaning you must train and store 24 models for a daily horizon or 168 models for a weekly horizon).

## Step 1: Analyze PACF to Find Candidate Lags

You can use the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) to identify which past steps have a direct statistical relationship with the future target. The `skforecast.stats` module provides a fast utility to calculate and rank these lags:

*   Calculate the lag table using `calculate_lag_autocorrelation`.
*   Filter for lags where the absolute partial autocorrelation is greater than the 95% confidence interval threshold, defined as `1.96 / sqrt(n)`, where `n` is the number of samples in your training set.
*   Use these significant lags as your initial `lags` configuration in your forecaster.

Here is a quick example of how to extract these candidate lags:

```python
import numpy as np
from skforecast.stats import calculate_lag_autocorrelation

# Rank lags by |PACF|

# Filter by the significance threshold

    lag_table
    .loc[lag_table['partial_autocorrelation_abs'] > threshold, 'lag']
    .astype(int)
    .sort_values()
    .tolist()
```
