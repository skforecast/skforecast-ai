### [grounded] For hourly demand with strong daily and weekly seasonality, when should I prefer a direct forecasting strategy over a recursive one?

- Skills: choosing-a-forecaster; 60.0s

Prefer a direct forecasting strategy (`ForecasterDirect`) when the predictive relationship changes significantly across the forecast horizon, or when recursive error propagation degrades accuracy over long horizons. For hourly demand, direct forecasting is particularly useful if predicting 1 hour ahead relies on different dynamics than predicting 24 or 168 hours ahead, and you want to prevent intermediate prediction errors from cascading forward.

## When to Choose Direct Forecasting

* Error accumulation across long horizons: A recursive forecaster feeds its own predictions back as lag inputs for subsequent steps, which can compound errors across long horizons such as 24-hour or 168-hour seasonal cycles. A direct forecaster trains an independent model for each step, conditioning exclusively on observed historical values.
* Horizon-dependent dynamics: If the influence of recent lags decays quickly while seasonal patterns dominate further out, step-specific models can capture those changing relationships without compromise.
* Fixed horizon requirements: Direct forecasting works well when your operating requirements have a predetermined, fixed horizon (such as day-ahead forecasting with 24 hourly steps), because `ForecasterDirect` requires `steps` to be set at initialization.

## Trade-offs to Consider

* Computational cost: Direct forecasting trains N separate estimators (one for each step in the horizon), which increases training time and memory footprint compared to the single estimator trained by `ForecasterRecursive`. You can mitigate training time using the `n_jobs` parameter for parallelization.
* Horizon flexibility: `ForecasterRecursive` can forecast any arbitrary number of steps dynamically at prediction time, whereas `ForecasterDirect` cannot predict beyond the horizon specified at initialization.

Because no dataset or forecast context was provided, the specific best number of lags for your series is not available. In time series modeling, the ideal number of lags depends on the sampling frequency, seasonal cycles, and autocorrelation dynamics of your specific data. You can identify an effective lag set by examining partial autocorrelation values or by evaluating lag candidates through hyperparameter tuning.

## Methods to Determine the Best Lags

* Inspect Partial Autocorrelation (PACF): The PACF measures the direct correlation between an observation and its past values after removing the effects of intermediate lags. Significant spikes in the PACF indicate which individual lags carry strong predictive signal.
* Use Domain and Seasonal Frequencies: Align your lag selection with natural cycles in your data. For example, daily data often benefits from weekly lags (such as 1 to 7, or multiples of 7), while hourly data frequently incorporates daily cycles (such as 24 or 48).
* Rank Lags with skforecast Tools: The `calculate_lag_autocorrelation` function in `skforecast.stats` calculates and ranks lags based on the absolute value of their partial autocorrelation.
* Search Over Candidates: Use hyperparameter optimization or backtesting to compare forecasters configured with different lag sets and select the one that yields the lowest validation error.

## Example Workflow Using skforecast

import numpy as np
from skforecast.stats import calculate_lag_autocorrelation
from skforecast.recursive import ForecasterRecursive
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor

# 1. Rank candidate lags by partial autocorrelation
lag_table = calculate_lag_autocorrelation(data=y_train, n_lags=24)

# 2. Select lags exceeding the 95% white-noise threshold
threshold = 1.96 / np.sqrt(len(y_train))
selected_lags = (
    lag_table
    .loc[lag_table["partial_autocorrelation_abs"] > threshold, "lag"]
    .astype(int)
    .sort_values()
    .tolist()
)

# 3. Initialize forecaster with the selected lags
forecaster = ForecasterRecursive(
    estimator=HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=963),
    lags=selected_lags,
)
forecaster.fit(y=y_train)
