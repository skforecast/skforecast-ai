### [grounded] For hourly demand with strong daily and weekly seasonality, when should I prefer a direct forecasting strategy over a recursive one?

- Skills: choosing-a-forecaster; 10.4s

Start with a recursive strategy (`ForecasterRecursive`), and prefer a direct one (`ForecasterDirect`) when the relationship between your inputs and the target looks like it changes across the forecast horizon, for example if predicting 1 hour ahead needs different patterns than predicting 24 hours ahead. Strong daily and weekly seasonality does not by itself call for direct; it is the horizon-dependent behavior, or error accumulation over long horizons, that does.

## When direct is the better fit

- **Step-specific relationships:** Direct trains one independent model per step, so each horizon can learn its own mapping from the observed data.
- **Avoiding error propagation:** Recursive feeds its own predictions back as inputs for later steps, so errors can compound. Direct uses only observed data for every step.
- **Long horizons:** The documentation describes direct as best for long horizons where patterns change per step, and recursive as best for most cases, especially short-to-medium horizons.

## Trade-offs to weigh

- **Number of models:** Recursive trains one model; direct trains one per step, so training is slower and memory use is higher (the estimators are stored in `estimators_`).
- **Parallelism:** Direct training can be parallelized with `n_jobs`.
- **Fixed horizon:** Direct requires `steps` at initialization, while recursive accepts any `steps` at prediction time.
- **Feature support:** Both support lags, window features (`RollingFeatures`), exogenous variables, and both bootstrapping and conformal prediction intervals.

### [probe] What is the best number of lags for my dataset?

- Skills: choosing-a-forecaster, autocorrelation-and-lag-selection; 9.0s

## How to get a data-driven starting point

- Use `calculate_lag_autocorrelation` from `skforecast.stats` to rank lags by absolute partial autocorrelation (PACF).
- Keep the lags whose PACF clears the asymptotic white-noise band, then pass them to the `lags` argument of your forecaster.
- Treat the result as an informed starting point, not a final answer.

```python
import numpy as np
from skforecast.stats import calculate_lag_autocorrelation
from skforecast.recursive import ForecasterRecursive
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor

lag_table = calculate_lag_autocorrelation(data=y_train, n_lags=24)

threshold = 1.96 / np.sqrt(len(y_train))
selected_lags = (
    lag_table
    .loc[lag_table['partial_autocorrelation_abs'] > threshold, 'lag']
    .astype(int)
    .sort_values()
    .tolist()
)

forecaster = ForecasterRecursive(
    estimator=HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=963),
    lags=selected_lags,
)
```

The `n_lags=24` value and the random state are illustrative. Choose `n_lags` to cover the seasonal period you expect in your data. It must be strictly less than half the series length.

## Reading the patterns

- If the PACF cuts off after lag p, lags 1 through p are the relevant ones.
- If the ACF shows peaks at multiples of some lag k, that suggests seasonality of period k, so lag k and its multiples are worth including.
- A strong trend can mask the structure at higher lags. In that case, read the ACF and PACF on the differenced series, or use the `differentiation` parameter in the forecaster.

## Cautions

- The confidence bands are pointwise, not joint tests. Several lags can cross the band by chance, so use them to spot candidates, not to prove significance.
- Autocorrelation shows how the series relates to its own past. It does not guarantee the lag set that gives the best forecast accuracy with your chosen estimator.
