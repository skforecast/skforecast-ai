### [grounded] For hourly demand with strong daily and weekly seasonality, when should I prefer a direct forecasting strategy over a recursive one?

- Skills: choosing-a-forecaster; 12.0s

Start with a recursive forecaster (`ForecasterRecursive`) and switch to a direct one (`ForecasterDirect`) when the relationship between your inputs and the target changes substantially across the forecast horizon. Strong daily and weekly seasonality does not by itself point to direct. No forecasting results were supplied, so I can't say which strategy fits your data better.

## When direct is the better fit

- **Horizon-dependent patterns:** A direct forecaster trains one independent model per step. This helps when, for example, predicting the next hour needs a different relationship than predicting a day ahead.
- **Error propagation:** A recursive forecaster feeds its own predictions back in as inputs for later steps, so errors can accumulate over long horizons. A direct forecaster uses only observed data at each step, so this does not occur.

## When recursive is the better fit

- **Default choice:** The documentation recommends `ForecasterRecursive` as the starting point for single-series problems. It works with any sklearn-compatible estimator and supports lags, window features, exogenous variables and differentiation.
- **Flexible horizon:** You can choose any number of steps at predict time, whereas direct fixes `steps` at initialization.
- **Lower cost:** Recursive trains one model and stores one estimator. Direct trains and stores one per step, which is slower and uses more memory, although training can be parallelized with `n_jobs`.
- **Short-to-medium horizons:** The documentation lists these as the typical use for recursive.

## What they share

Both strategies support lags, window features (`RollingFeatures`), exogenous variables, and both bootstrapping and conformal prediction intervals. Daily and weekly seasonal signals can be encoded in either one, for instance with calendar features as exogenous variables, so the choice rests on the horizon behavior above rather than on feature support.

The documentation does not give a rule that maps a specific seasonal structure to one strategy. I can't validate from here whether step-specific patterns exist in your demand series. The reliable way to find out is to backtest both with the same `TimeSeriesFold` and compare their metrics.

## Get a candidate set of lags

`calculate_lag_autocorrelation` ranks lags by absolute partial autocorrelation. The values below are illustrative; replace them with your own.

```python
import numpy as np
from skforecast.stats import calculate_lag_autocorrelation

lag_table = calculate_lag_autocorrelation(
    data=y_train,
    n_lags=24,
    sort_by='partial_autocorrelation_abs',
)

threshold = 1.96 / np.sqrt(len(y_train))
selected_lags = (
    lag_table
    .loc[lag_table['partial_autocorrelation_abs'] > threshold, 'lag']
    .astype(int)
    .sort_values()
    .tolist()
)
```

Pass `selected_lags` to the `lags` argument of your forecaster, for example `ForecasterRecursive`.

## How to read the patterns

- If the PACF cuts off after lag p, lags 1 through p are the relevant ones.
- If the ACF peaks at lags k, 2k, 3k, the series may have seasonality of period k, and lags around that period may be worth including.
- If the series has a visible trend, read the ACF and PACF on the differenced series, or use the forecaster's `differentiation` parameter.
- The confidence bands are pointwise, so some lags will cross them by chance. Use them to spot candidates, not as formal tests.
- For PACF, `n_lags` must be strictly less than half the series length.

## Treat it as a starting point

The lags from the PACF are an informed guess, not a final answer. To refine them:

- Use `bayesian_search_forecaster` (see the hyperparameter-optimization skill), which can include `lags` in its search space and scores candidates with cross-validation.
- Use `select_features` to prune redundant lags once the forecaster is configured.
- If you use the skforecast assistant, `assistant.compare()` can help you choose between candidate models, and `assistant.backtest()` measures the accuracy of a chosen configuration.
