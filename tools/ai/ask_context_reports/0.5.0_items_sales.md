### [grounded] For hourly demand with strong daily and weekly seasonality, when should I prefer a direct forecasting strategy over a recursive one?

- Skills: choosing-a-forecaster; 11.0s

Start with a recursive strategy (`ForecasterRecursive`) and move to a direct one (`ForecasterDirect`) when the relationship between your inputs and the target changes meaningfully across the forecast horizon. Strong daily and weekly seasonality does not by itself favor either strategy, and I have no results for your data, so I can't say which fits it better.

## When direct is the better fit

- **Horizon-dependent patterns:** The documentation describes the case where predicting at 1 hour ahead and at 24 hours ahead need different relationships. `ForecasterDirect` trains one independent model per step, so each horizon can learn its own mapping.
- **Avoiding error propagation:** A recursive forecaster feeds its own predictions back as inputs for later steps. A direct forecaster uses only observed data for every step, so errors do not compound that way.
- **Long horizons:** The documentation lists long horizons with step-specific patterns as the best use case for direct. It lists most other cases, especially short-to-medium horizons, as better suited to recursive.

## When recursive is the better default

- It is the general-purpose starting point: one model, and any `steps` value can be chosen at predict time.
- It trains faster and uses less memory, since it stores one estimator rather than one per step.

## Practical trade-offs of direct

- `steps` must be fixed when you create the forecaster, so you cannot change the horizon freely at predict time.
- It trains N models (one per step), which is slower and stores more estimators. Training can be parallelized with `n_jobs`.
- Both strategies support bootstrapping and conformal prediction intervals, so intervals do not favor either one.

## Caveat

The documentation gives no rule for which strategy wins on a given seasonal pattern, and I have no backtest results for your demand series. Treat the choice as something to verify empirically rather than assume.

### [probe] What is the best number of lags for my dataset?

- Skills: choosing-a-forecaster, autocorrelation-and-lag-selection; 9.7s

I can't give a specific best number of lags because no dataset or forecasting results were provided with your question, and no single value is best for every series. The documented approach is to get a data-driven starting set of lags from the partial autocorrelation (PACF), then refine it with cross-validation.

## Step 1: Get candidate lags from the PACF

`calculate_lag_autocorrelation` in `skforecast.stats` ranks lags by absolute partial autocorrelation. You can keep the lags that exceed the asymptotic white-noise band. The values below (such as `n_lags=24`) are illustrative; choose them to suit your data.

```python
import numpy as np
from skforecast.stats import calculate_lag_autocorrelation
from skforecast.recursive import ForecasterRecursive
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor

lag_table = calculate_lag_autocorrelation(data=y_train, n_lags=24)

threshold = 1.96 / np.sqrt(len(y_train))
selected_lags = (
    lag_table
    .loc[lag_table['partial_autocorrelation_abs'] > threshold, 'lag']
    .astype(int)
    .sort_values()
    .tolist()
)

forecaster = ForecasterRecursive(
    estimator=HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=963),
    lags=selected_lags,
)
```

## Step 2: Read the patterns

- If the PACF cuts off after lag *p*, lags 1 to *p* are the relevant ones.
- If the autocorrelation peaks at lags *k*, *2k*, *3k*, the series has seasonality of period *k*, so lags around those multiples are worth including.
- If the autocorrelation decays slowly at every lag, there may be a trend. Read the ACF and PACF on the differenced series, or use the `differentiation` parameter of the forecaster.

## Step 3: Treat the result as a starting point

- The confidence bands are pointwise, not joint tests, so some lags will cross the band by chance. Use them to spot candidates, not to formally decide.
- `pacf` requires `nlags` to be less than half the series length.
- The selected lags are an informed starting point, not a final answer. To compare configurations by cross-validation, use `bayesian_search_forecaster`, which can include `lags` in its search space. To prune redundant lags after the forecaster is set up, use `select_features`.
