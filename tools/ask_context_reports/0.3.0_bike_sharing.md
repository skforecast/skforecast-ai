### [grounded] For hourly demand with strong daily and weekly seasonality, when should I prefer a direct forecasting strategy over a recursive one?

- Skills: choosing-a-forecaster; 14.0s

For hourly demand with strong daily and weekly seasonality, you should prefer a direct forecasting strategy when you need to avoid error propagation over long horizons or when the predictive relationship changes significantly across the forecast horizon. Because a direct forecaster trains an independent model for each step, it is highly effective at capturing step-specific patterns without letting early-step prediction errors compound over a 24-hour or 168-hour cycle. 

Here is a breakdown of when to select a direct strategy over a recursive one for this use case.

## When to Choose a Direct Strategy

* **To Avoid Error Propagation**: In recursive forecasting, a model uses its own previous predictions as inputs for subsequent steps. Over long horizons, such as predicting 24 hours or 168 hours of demand, these errors can compound and distort the seasonal profile. A direct strategy trains independent models for each step, ensuring that early errors do not propagate.
* **Horizon-Dependent Relationships**: If the predictive relationship changes significantly across the forecast horizon, a direct forecaster is ideal. For example, predicting demand at hour 1 may require different patterns and relationships than predicting demand at hour 24.
* **Capturing Specific Seasonal Offsets**: Since each step is modeled independently, the estimator for a specific hour (like step 24 or step 168) can optimize its weights specifically to leverage the corresponding daily or weekly seasonal lags.

## Trade-offs to Consider

* **Computational Resources**: A direct strategy trains one model per forecast step. For a 24-hour horizon, this means training 24 models, which requires more memory and training time than a single recursive model (though this can be parallelized using the `n_jobs` parameter).
* **Horizon Flexibility**: You must define your exact forecast horizon (the `steps` parameter) at initialization. Unlike a recursive forecaster, which can predict any arbitrary number of steps at predict time, a direct forecaster cannot predict beyond the number of steps configured during its setup.

Here are the two primary ways to find the best lags for your dataset.

## Method 1: Autocorrelation Analysis (PACF)

The Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF) measures the direct correlation between a time series and its past values, removing the influence of intermediate lags. Lags with a high absolute PACF value are strong candidates for your model.

You can calculate and rank these lags using skforecast's built-in statistical utilities:

```python
import numpy as np
from skforecast.stats import calculate_lag_autocorrelation
from skforecast.recursive import ForecasterRecursive
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor

# Rank lags 1 to 24 by their absolute PACF value
lag_table = calculate_lag_autocorrelation(data=y_train, n_lags=24)

# Filter lags that exceed the 95% confidence interval threshold
threshold = 1.96 / np.sqrt(len(y_train))
selected_lags = (
    lag_table
    .loc[lag_table['partial_autocorrelation_abs'] > threshold, 'lag']
    .astype(int)
    .sort_values()
    .tolist()
)

# Use these selected lags in your forecaster
forecaster = ForecasterRecursive(
    estimator=HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=963),
    lags=selected_lags,
)
```

## Method 2: Hyperparameter Tuning and Backtesting

While PACF analysis provides a strong statistical starting point, the absolute "best" lags are often determined empirically by testing how different configurations perform on unseen data. 

You can use skforecast's hyperparameter tuning functions (such as `grid_search_forecaster` or `bayesian_search_forecaster`) to search across different lag lists and evaluate them using backtesting. This process allows you to:

* Compare a continuous range of lags (e.g., lags 1 to 7) against a sparse set of lags (e.g., lags 1, 2, 7, and 14).
* Jointly optimize the lag configuration and the machine learning model's hyperparameters (like learning rate or max depth).
* Select the lags that produce the lowest validation error (such as Mean Absolute Error or Root Mean Squared Error) over your backtesting folds.
